# First Customer Playbook

How to sell Bindersnap by hand before writing more code.

This document is operational, not aspirational. It defines exactly who to
contact, what to say, how to run the calls, what to charge, and what has to be
true before going back to the editor.

The premise: **you are not launching a product. You are hand-selling three
customers.** Everything below is sized for that. Nothing here scales, and that
is the point.

---

## 0. The one decision that changes everything

The landing page promises "No demo call, no implementation project." That is the
right promise for the product at scale and the wrong motion for customer number
one.

Self-serve signup works when strangers already trust the category and can
evaluate you alone. Nobody has heard of Bindersnap, the buyer is a non-technical
administrator in a risk-averse industry, and the product asks them to move the
document set their accreditation depends on. They will not do that from a
landing page.

So: **sell by hand, deliver self-serve.** You get on calls, you load their
policies yourself, you give them your cell number. The product stays
self-serve — that is what makes the eventual business work — but the first
three customers are closed by a person, not a funnel. Don't change the landing
page to add "Book a demo." Change what you personally do on Monday morning.

## 1. Who to contact — the ICP, formally

### The segment

US ambulatory healthcare providers, **10–150 employees**, accredited or
pursuing accreditation, with **no dedicated IT staff** and **no incumbent policy
management system**.

Four sub-verticals, in priority order:

| #   | Sub-vertical                                             | Typical accreditor              | Why they fit                                                                                            | Where the list is                                                                                           |
| --- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | **Ambulatory surgery centers (ASCs)**                    | AAAHC, AAAASF, Joint Commission | Small admin team, real survey pressure, 100–300 policies, a single administrator with signing authority | CMS Provider of Services file (data.cms.gov); state licensure lists; AAAHC "find an organization" directory |
| 2   | **Home health & hospice agencies**                       | CHAP, ACHC, Joint Commission    | Same pressure plus high staff turnover, so "who approved this" breaks constantly                        | CMS Care Compare provider data (data.cms.gov); state home care association member directories               |
| 3   | **Behavioral health / SUD programs**                     | CARF, Joint Commission BHC, COA | Growing fast, often multi-site, policy manual is a genuine accreditation artifact                       | CARF and COA public directories; SAMHSA facility locator                                                    |
| 4   | **Community clinics / FQHCs, urgent care, IDD services** | HRSA OSV, AAAHC                 | Same shape, slightly slower buying                                                                      | HRSA health center directory; state primary care association rosters                                        |

Start with ASCs. They have the sharpest survey moment, the smallest number of
decision makers, and the highest density of "we run this on a shared drive."

Pull the real counts from the CMS files when you download them — you need the
list anyway, and it will give you exact numbers rather than my estimates.

### The buyer

One person is champion, decision maker, budget holder, and daily user. That is
the whole reason this segment is winnable.

**Titles to target, in order:**

1. Administrator / Practice Administrator / Executive Director
2. Director of Nursing (DON) / Clinical Director
3. Quality Coordinator / QAPI Coordinator / Performance Improvement Manager
4. Compliance Officer / Accreditation Coordinator (larger orgs only)
5. Owner / Managing Partner (physician-owned ASCs)

**What is on their desk:** a policy manual of 80–300 documents in Word, living
in a shared drive or a binder or both. An accreditation survey on a one- to
three-year cycle. A staff that asks "is this the current one?" A predecessor who
left no record. No budget line for software and no patience for a procurement
process.

**What they control:** a credit card or a purchasing card with a discretionary
limit that comfortably clears $100/month. This is the single most important fact
in this document. Your price sits below the threshold where anyone else has to
be asked.

### Disqualify immediately

Say no to these politely and fast. Each one costs you a month and teaches you
nothing.

- **Hospitals and health systems.** Procurement, security review, a vendor risk
  questionnaire, a nine-month cycle, and they already own PolicyStat or
  PolicyTech.
- **Anyone who wants PHI in the system.** Policies and procedures are not
  patient records. The moment a prospect wants to store anything with patient
  data in it, you are in a different product and a different regulatory posture.
- **Anyone who needs EHR integration**, SSO/SAML, or an on-premise install.
- **Anyone with a live contract** with an incumbent policy system, unless they
  are within 90 days of renewal and volunteering that they are unhappy.
- **Life sciences, medical device, pharma.** They will ask for 21 CFR Part 11
  validated e-signatures. You do not have that and should not claim it.

### Trigger events — rank your list by these

A prospect with a trigger converts several times better than an identical
prospect without one.

1. **A survey window opening in the next 3–9 months.** The single strongest
   signal. Accreditation cycles are semi-public — ask on the call.
2. **A recent deficiency citation.** CMS statements of deficiency are public
   for many provider types. A center that was just cited on policy currency has
   budget, urgency, and a mandate.
3. **A new administrator or DON in the last 6 months.** They inherited the mess,
   they are not defending the status quo, and they are actively looking to fix
   something visible in their first year. Track this with LinkedIn job-change
   alerts — it is the highest-ROI free tool you have.
4. **A new site or de novo center opening.** They are building a policy manual
   from nothing. No migration objection at all.
5. **An incumbent price increase at renewal.** Policy management vendors have
   consolidated; renewal quotes go up. Angry renewal is your best cold-email
   angle after triggers 1–3.

## 2. Where the names come from

Ranked by cost per first customer, not by volume.

### Tier 0 — your wife's network. This is where customer #1 comes from.

You have something almost no founder has: a domain expert at home whose whole
professional network is your ICP, and an origin story that is literally true.

Do this in week one:

- Sit down with her and write out **20 named people**: current colleagues,
  former colleagues, the compliance person at the last place she worked, the
  DON at the sister facility, the consultant who ran their last accreditation
  prep, the rep from the system they already hate.
- For each: name, org, title, and one sentence on why they would care.
- Ask her for **five introductions**, not twenty. Introductions cost her social
  capital; spend it deliberately on the five with the strongest triggers.
- Ask her to forward the landing page to one group chat or professional Slack
  or Facebook group of peers, with her own words, not yours.

Twenty warm names beats five hundred cold ones. Do not skip this to go do
something that feels more like "marketing."

### Tier 1 — accreditation consultants. This is where customers #2 through #10 come from.

There is a whole profession of independent consultants who prepare small
providers for AAAHC / ACHC / CHAP / CARF surveys. They arrive at an agency,
find the policy manual in disarray, and rebuild it. **They do the exact work
your product does, by hand, for a living, across many clients.**

They are not competitors. They are your distribution.

- Find them: LinkedIn search for "accreditation consultant" plus the accreditor
  name; the speaker lists of state association conferences; the "resources" page
  of accreditor websites; ask every prospect "who helped you with your last
  survey?"
- The pitch to them is different from the pitch to a provider: _"You rebuild
  policy manuals for a living. I built the tool you'd want your clients to keep
  using after you leave. I'll give you a free account for your own use, and
  I'll pay you a referral fee on anyone who subscribes."_
- Offer something real: 20% recurring for 12 months, or a flat $200 per closed
  referral. At $100/month either is trivial to honor and meaningful to them.
- Target **five consultant relationships**, not fifty. One good one sends you
  three clients a year forever.

### Tier 2 — cold outreach against public lists

This is the volume channel and the slowest. Do it, but do not do it first.

- Download the CMS provider files. Filter to your sub-vertical and state.
- Enrich with a name and title. The org website's "About" or "Leadership" page,
  or LinkedIn, gets you there. Budget 3–5 minutes per contact; do not buy a list.
- **Personalize on one specific fact per email.** The center's name, their
  accreditor, a recent expansion, a new administrator. Generic mail merge in
  this segment gets zero replies and burns your domain.
- Send from a subdomain (`mail.bindersnap.com` or similar) with SPF/DKIM/DMARC
  configured, not from your primary domain. Keep it under 30 a day.

### Tier 3 — associations and conferences

State ASC associations and state home care associations have member
directories, email newsletters that accept sponsors cheaply, and small regional
conferences where a $500–2,000 table puts you in front of exactly your ICP for
two days.

This is worth doing after you have one reference customer, not before. Go to
one as an attendee first, with no booth, and have twenty conversations.

### Deliberately not doing yet

Paid ads, SEO and content marketing, Product Hunt, posting on LinkedIn for
reach, cold calling, a partner program page, a webinar. Every one of these is a
way to feel busy without talking to a buyer. Revisit after customer #3.

## 3. The outreach

### Warm introduction request (what you send _her colleague's_ contact, via her)

> Subject: quick question about your policy manual
>
> Hi [Name] — [Wife] mentioned you run [role] at [Org].
>
> I'm building something for policy manuals at places like yours and I'm trying
> to find out where I'm wrong before I go any further. Not selling you anything
> — I genuinely want the 15 minutes of "here's what actually happens at survey."
>
> Would you be open to a call this week or next?
>
> David

Note what this does not contain: a product description, a link, a demo offer,
or a benefit claim. The ask is for their expertise, which is easy to say yes to
and gets you the same meeting.

### Cold email (Tier 2)

Four sentences. One question. No attachment, no calendar link in the first
email.

> Subject: [Center Name]'s policy manual before the AAAHC survey
>
> [Name] — I build software, and my wife came home frustrated with her policy
> system often enough that I started building a replacement.
>
> One question, and I'm not going to pitch you: when your last survey asked for
> the approved version of a policy, how long did it take to show who signed off
> and on which draft?
>
> If the answer was "longer than it should have been," I'd like 15 minutes to
> hear how it actually went.
>
> David Gray
> bindersnap.com

Follow-up sequence — three touches, then stop:

- **+4 days:** one line. _"[Name] — bumping this once in case it got buried. Still curious how your last survey went."_
- **+10 days:** change the angle, add value. Send them something genuinely
  useful — a short checklist of what surveyors ask for on policy currency —
  with no ask attached.
- **+21 days:** the breakup. _"I'll stop here — if policy approvals are ever the thing keeping you up before a survey, my door's open."_ This one gets replies surprisingly often.

### Consultant partner email (Tier 1)

> Subject: the policy manual cleanup you do by hand
>
> [Name] — you prep agencies for [CHAP/ACHC/AAAHC] surveys, so you've rebuilt
> more policy manuals than anyone I could talk to.
>
> I built a system where every policy carries its own approval record — who
> signed off, when, on exactly which version — so the manual doesn't drift back
> into a shared drive after you leave.
>
> I'd like to give you a free account to poke holes in it, and if you ever think
> it fits a client, I pay a referral fee. Worth 20 minutes?
>
> David

## 4. The calls — run two, not one

The most common founder mistake is demoing on the first call. Do not. You
cannot demo well until you know which of their problems to point the demo at,
and the demo ends the conversation you actually needed to have.

### Call 1 — discovery (20 minutes, no screen share)

Open by lowering the stakes: _"I'm not going to show you anything today. I want
to understand how policy approvals actually work at [Org], and if it turns out
this isn't a problem for you, that's a completely fine outcome for me."_

Then ask about the past, never the future. Every question below is about
something that already happened, which is the only kind of answer you can trust.

**The questions, in order:**

1. "Walk me through the last policy that got approved. Start from who wrote it."
2. "Where does it live right now? What's the file called?"
3. "How does someone on staff know it's the current one?"
4. "Who signed off on it? How would you show me that?"
5. "When was your last survey? What did they ask for on policies?"
6. "Was anything cited?"
7. "What did the week before the survey look like for you?"
8. "Roughly how many policies, and what format are they in?"
9. "Who else touches them — reviewers, medical director, board?"
10. "What have you tried to fix this? Did you pay for anything?"
11. "If nothing changes, what happens at the next survey?"
12. "Who else would have to be involved in a decision like this?"

**Never ask:** "Would you use this?" "Would you pay for this?" "Does that sound
useful?" These generate polite lies. Ask what they have already done and already
paid for.

**Score the call, 1 point each. Three or more, pursue:**

- Survey within the next 9 months
- Has been cited, or nearly was, on policy currency
- More than 50 policies
- Has already paid for something to fix this (templates, a consultant, a system)
- Can spend $100/month without asking another person

**Close call 1 with a concrete next step and homework:** _"Send me two of your
actual policies — the messiest ones — and I'll load them in and show you your
own manual next week, not a demo account."_

That homework is the highest-conversion move in this entire playbook. A prospect
who sends you files has invested. A demo on their own documents lands about ten
times harder than one on `Infection_Control_Policy_rev3_FINAL(2).docx`.

### Call 2 — the demo (30 minutes, 7 minutes of screen)

Load their two policies before the call. Run the storyline in this order — it
is the survey moment, not a feature tour.

1. **Start at the end.** Open the activity record. _"This is what you hand a
   surveyor."_ Sixty seconds, and it is the whole value proposition.
2. **Open one of their policies.** The current approved version, who approved
   it, when, which version.
3. **Make a change.** Upload their revised Word file as a change request.
4. **Show the comparison.** The additions and deletions marked inside the
   rendered policy — not two columns of source. This is the moment they lean in,
   because every other system they have seen makes them read raw text.
5. **Approve it.** The version locks. Say the sentence plainly: _"You cannot
   change an approved policy without creating a new version somebody has to
   review. That's the whole product."_
6. **Return to the record.** Show the new entry. _"That's the loop. It's the
   same loop every time, and it builds the record for you."_

**Do not show:** signup, billing, settings, collaborator management, the inline
editor, or anything on the roadmap. Every extra screen is a chance to raise an
objection about something that is not the reason they would buy.

## 5. The pitch, in words

### The 30-second version (for anyone, anywhere)

> You know how in a clinic the policy manual lives in a shared drive, and when
> the surveyor asks who approved a policy and when, somebody spends a weekend
> reconstructing it from email? Bindersnap gives every policy its own approval
> record — who signed off, on exactly which version, permanently. It's a hundred
> dollars a month and you can load your manual in an afternoon.

### The two-minute version (the discovery-call answer to "so what is it?")

> There are three parts to a policy: writing it, getting it approved, and
> proving it was approved. Everybody has the first part solved — it's Word. The
> second part is an email chain. The third part doesn't exist, so it gets
> reconstructed under pressure at survey time.
>
> Bindersnap is where the second and third parts live. You upload the policies
> you already have — Word, PDF, whatever they are now. A change to any policy
> becomes a request that named people approve. When they approve it, that
> version locks, and the record of who approved what and when is permanent and
> can't be edited after the fact.
>
> The thing that makes it different from a shared drive with good intentions is
> that the record isn't a feature you have to remember to use. It's a
> consequence of how the system works. You can't change an approved policy
> without creating a new version somebody reviews.
>
> Engineers have worked this way for thirty years. I just built it for the
> people who keep policy manuals.

### The before / after, if you need one slide

|                                 | Today                                                                | With Bindersnap                  |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Where the current version lives | A shared drive folder, plus somebody's desktop                       | One place, marked current        |
| How you know it's current       | You ask around                                                       | The system says so               |
| The approval                    | An email chain, sometimes in a mailbox belonging to someone who left | A permanent record on the policy |
| Survey week                     | A weekend of reconstruction                                          | Open the record                  |
| Changing an approved policy     | Anyone with the file can                                             | Requires a new reviewed version  |

## 6. The ask, and the close

### Price

**$100/month, flat, for the whole organization.** Do not discount it, do not
invent an annual plan, and do not build tiers. The price is already correct for
this stage for three reasons: it is under the buyer's discretionary limit, it is
obviously cheaper than the incumbents, and it is high enough that paying it
proves the problem is real.

Get an incumbent quote yourself — have a peer or your wife request pricing from
one of the established policy management vendors. Knowing their real number
turns your price into a fact you can state rather than a claim.

### The founding customer offer

Make it explicit, time-boxed, and generous with your labor rather than your
price.

> **Founding customer terms**
>
> - $100/month, and this price is locked for as long as you stay a customer.
> - I load your existing policy manual myself, before you pay anything.
> - You have my cell number.
> - Every feature request from the first three customers goes to the front of
>   the queue, and I'll tell you honestly what I won't build.
> - Cancel any time. If you cancel, I export every policy and every approval
>   record and hand them to you in a format you can keep.

### Do not run a free pilot

A free pilot with no commitment produces a customer who never logs in and never
tells you the truth. If someone will not pay $100 after the demo, the problem is
not the price. It is that the pain is not real for them, or you have not reached
the person who feels it. Find that out in week two rather than month four.

The one acceptable variant: **a paid pilot** — 30 days at $100, cancel any time,
you do the import. That is not a discount; it is the normal product with your
labor added.

### Asking for the card

Do it on the demo call, out loud, and then stop talking.

> _"Here's what I'd suggest. I'll load your manual — send me the folder and give
> me a few days. Once it's in and you've looked at it, it's $100 a month and you
> can cancel any time. Do you want me to start on the import?"_

If they say yes: get the folder before the call ends. Momentum dies in the gap.

If they hesitate, find the real blocker with one question: _"What would have to
be true for this to be an easy yes?"_

## 7. Objections, with honest answers

**"Is this HIPAA compliant? Will you sign a BAA?"**

Policies and procedures do not contain patient information, so this generally
sits outside the business-associate question entirely — but do not offer a
compliance opinion, and do not claim a certification you do not have. Say what
is true: what the system stores, where it runs, who can access it. Then:
_"If your compliance officer requires a BAA regardless of content, tell me now
and I'll give you a straight answer about whether I can sign one today."_

**Decide before your first call whether you will sign a BAA.** Signing one you
cannot honor is far worse than losing the deal. If the answer is not yet, the
right response is to say so and disqualify gracefully.

**"What happens to our policies if you go out of business?"**

The most legitimate question a small vendor gets, and you have an unusually good
answer because everything is stored as an ordinary git repository. _"Your
documents and the entire approval history export in a standard format, any time,
and I'll write that into the agreement."_ Then make sure that is true — see the
product gaps below.

**"We already use [an established policy system]."**

Disqualify unless they volunteer unhappiness or a renewal within 90 days. Ask
one question — _"if you were starting over, would you buy it again?"_ — and
respect the answer. Do not run a displacement campaign as a solo founder.

**"Can people sign electronically? Is this 21 CFR Part 11?"**

_"It records who approved which version and when, in a history that can't be
rewritten after the fact. It is not a Part 11 validated e-signature system, and
I won't tell you it is."_ For accreditation policy approval this is normally
what is wanted; for FDA-regulated manufacturing it is not, and that prospect is
outside your ICP anyway.

**"I need to run it past the board / my medical director / the owner."**

Usually true and usually fine at this price. Convert it into a date and a
deliverable: _"Makes sense. What would help you make that case? I can put
together a one-page summary of what changes for you at survey time."_ Then send
it within 24 hours.

**"Our survey isn't for two years."**

Weak trigger, but not dead. Reframe to the daily cost: _"The survey is when it
hurts most, but the version confusion is every week. How often does someone ask
whether they've got the current copy?"_ If that lands flat, park them politely
and move on.

**"Can it do [training attestation / competency tracking / incident reports]?"**

No, and say so cleanly. _"No — that's a different product and I'd rather do one
thing properly."_ Write the request down. A pattern across three prospects is a
roadmap item; one prospect's wish list is a distraction.

## 8. What you do by hand

These are deliberate, temporary, and they are how you win.

- **You do the import.** Their 80–300 Word files, into their workspace, before
  they pay. Do it with a script against the Gitea API — `tests/seed.ts` already
  drives that API and is the pattern to copy. Do not build an import UI for
  customer number one.
- **You convert legacy formats.** The comparison view renders `.docx` and PDF,
  not legacy `.doc`, `.xlsx`, or `.pptx`. Ask on the discovery call what format
  the manual is in, and convert `.doc` to `.docx` yourself during onboarding
  without mentioning it.
- **You check in weekly, by phone, for the first 90 days.** Not an email
  campaign. A call. Ask what they did in the system and what annoyed them.
- **You give them your cell number.** It is the strongest differentiator you
  have against a vendor with a support portal, and it costs nothing until you
  have twenty customers, at which point it is a good problem.

## 9. The product gaps that will actually block a sale

Found by reading the shipped code, in priority order. Only one of these needs
code before you can sell.

**1. Every reviewer needs their own paid subscription — fix before your first
demo.**

Subscriptions are keyed to a username (`services/api/subscriptions.ts`), and
`requireSubscription` gates the workspace routes in `services/api/server.ts`.
An administrator who subscribes and then invites their DON to approve a policy
will watch that DON hit a paywall. That kills the demo and the deal, and the
approval workflow is the entire product.

There is an admin access-override path already in the code
(`subscriptionAccessOverrides`, `resolveAccess`, and the admin subscription
management page). **Verify it end to end before your first demo**, and use it to
grant access to every reviewer at a founding customer. The real fix is
organization-level billing, which is already designed in ADR 0004 — but that is
post-first-customer work. Do not build it now; use the override.

**2. Export of the policies and the approval record — promise it now, build it
before you take money.**

There is no export path in the app today. You need this for the "what if you
disappear" objection, and a surveyor-ready export is arguably the product's
whole point. For the first customer you can produce it by hand from the git
repository, and that is a legitimate answer — but do not put a guarantee in
writing that you cannot honor within a day.

**3. Bulk import — do not build this.**

`UploadModal` takes a single file (`event.target.files?.[0]`). A real customer
has hundreds of policies. This looks like a blocker and is not, because you are
doing the import yourself with a script. Build the UI when a customer asks to
add fifty policies without you, which will be around customer three.

Everything else — the inline editor, real-time collaboration, the workspace and
folder model from ADR 0004 — is not blocking a sale and should not be touched
until a paying customer asks for it by name.

## 10. The 30-day plan

**Week 1 — build the list, get the first five conversations booked.**

- Sit down with your wife; write the 20 warm names. Ask for five introductions.
- Download the CMS provider file for your state and the two adjacent ones.
  Filter to ASCs. Enrich 50 contacts by hand.
- Identify 10 accreditation consultants.
- Verify the reviewer access-override path works end to end.
- Send: 5 warm intro requests, 10 consultant emails, 25 cold emails.
- **Target: 5 discovery calls booked.**

**Week 2 — run discovery. Do not demo.**

- Run every call that books. Take notes verbatim, in their words.
- Send 30 more cold emails; work the follow-up sequence on week one's sends.
- Ask every qualified prospect for two of their real policies.
- After call five, write down the three sentences you heard most often. Those
  sentences are your new landing page copy.
- **Target: 5+ discovery calls done, 3 prospects scoring 3+, 2 sets of real policies in hand.**

**Week 3 — demo on their documents, and ask.**

- Load each prospect's policies before their call.
- Run the 7-minute storyline. Ask for the card on the call.
- Keep sending: 30 cold emails, follow-ups, 5 more consultant conversations.
- **Target: 3 demos, 1 verbal yes.**

**Week 4 — close, onboard, and be honest about the result.**

- Import the first customer's full manual yourself.
- Grant reviewer access overrides. Watch them use it. Write down every point of
  friction.
- Get the payment live. A verbal yes is not a customer.
- **Target: 1 paying customer, or a clear written account of why not.**

## 11. The scoreboard

Five numbers. Track them in one spreadsheet, updated daily. Nothing else.

| Metric                                                          | 30-day target |
| --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------- |
| Qualified conversations (discovery calls with the actual buyer) | 15            |
| Prospects scoring 3+ on the qualification checklist             | 5             |
| Demos on the prospect's own documents                           | 5             |
| Paying customers                                                | 1             |
| Reply rate on cold email                                        | 8%+           |

Rough funnel math for cold outreach in this segment: 100 researched, personalized
emails produce roughly 8–15 replies, 4–6 calls, and 1–2 serious prospects.
Warm introductions run several times better. That is why Tier 0 and Tier 1 come
first — not because cold email does not work, but because it is the most
expensive way to buy your first conversation.

## 12. When to stop selling and go back to code

Set the exit condition now, while you are calm, rather than in week five when
you are tired of the phone.

**Go back to code when all three are true:**

1. One customer is paying and has used the system for something real —
   an actual policy approved by an actual reviewer, not a test document.
2. Fifteen discovery calls are done and the same three complaints appear in most
   of them. That repetition is the signal that you have learned the segment.
3. You have a written list of what blocked each lost deal, and at least one item
   appears more than twice. That item is your next sprint, and it will not be
   the feature you would have guessed.

**Change direction if, after 15 calls with the right titles:**

- Nobody has been burned by a version question. Then the pain is elsewhere and
  the positioning is wrong — but the calls will have told you where.
- Everybody agrees it is a real problem and nobody will pay $100. Then you are
  reaching the person who suffers but not the person who buys, and you need to
  move up one title.
- Every serious prospect requires a BAA or a security review. Then your ICP is
  larger than you thought and the next work is trust infrastructure, not
  features.

None of these outcomes is failure. All of them are answers you cannot get from
the editor.

## 13. What not to do

- Do not build anything a prospect has not asked for by name during a call.
- Do not add "Book a demo" to the landing page. Do the demos, keep the promise.
- Do not discount below $100 or invent an annual plan to close a deal.
- Do not run an unpaid pilot.
- Do not chase hospitals because the logo would look good.
- Do not claim HIPAA compliance, a BAA, SOC 2, or Part 11 you do not have. One
  overclaim in this industry ends the company's reputation in a referral network
  that talks constantly.
- Do not spend week one redesigning the landing page. You will rewrite the copy
  in week two anyway, using sentences your prospects said out loud.
