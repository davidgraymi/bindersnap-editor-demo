Every employer with staff who could reasonably be exposed to blood or other potentially infectious materials must have a written **exposure control plan** under OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens standard, [29 CFR 1910.1030](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-1910.1030). The plan has to say who is exposed, how you protect them, and what happens after an exposure. It must be **reviewed and updated at least annually**.

The template below follows the order of the standard, so an inspector can find each required element. Replace everything in **[brackets]**.

## What OSHA requires the plan to contain

Under §1910.1030(c)(1)(ii), the plan must contain at least:

1. **The exposure determination**: the job classifications in which all employees have occupational exposure, those in which some do, and the tasks that cause the exposure (§1910.1030(c)(2)).
2. **The schedule and method of implementation** for methods of compliance (engineering and work practice controls, personal protective equipment, housekeeping), hepatitis B vaccination and post-exposure follow-up, communication of hazards and training, and recordkeeping.
3. **The procedure for evaluating the circumstances** surrounding exposure incidents.

The annual review must also reflect changes in technology that reduce exposure, and **document annually the consideration and implementation** of safer medical devices (§1910.1030(c)(1)(iv)). You must ask non-managerial employees who give direct patient care for input on choosing those devices (§1910.1030(c)(1)(v)).

## The template

### [Organization name] Exposure Control Plan

- **Policy owner:** [Title, for example Infection Prevention Lead]
- **Approved by:** [Names and titles]
- **Effective date:** [Date]
- **Last reviewed:** [Date]

### 1. Purpose

This plan protects employees of [Organization name] from occupational exposure to bloodborne pathogens, including hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), in accordance with 29 CFR 1910.1030.

### 2. Responsibilities

- **[Program administrator title]** maintains, reviews and updates this plan at least annually and whenever tasks, procedures or positions change.
- **Managers and supervisors** make sure staff in their areas follow this plan and complete training.
- **Employees** follow the controls in this plan, use the protective equipment provided, and report every exposure incident immediately.

### 3. Exposure determination

**Job classifications in which all employees have occupational exposure:**
[For example: registered nurses, licensed practical nurses, medical assistants, phlebotomists, surgical technologists, physicians, advanced practice providers]

**Job classifications in which some employees have occupational exposure**, and the tasks that cause it:

| Job classification       | Tasks and procedures with exposure                |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| [Front desk staff]       | [Receiving specimens from patients]               |
| [Environmental services] | [Cleaning blood spills, handling regulated waste] |

The exposure determination is made without regard to the use of personal protective equipment.

### 4. Methods of compliance

**Universal precautions.** All blood and other potentially infectious materials are treated as infectious.

**Engineering controls.** [Organization name] uses [sharps containers at point of use, needleless systems, safety-engineered needles and lancets, self-sheathing devices]. Sharps containers are closable, puncture-resistant, leakproof, labeled and replaced before they are overfilled. [Title] inspects engineering controls [frequency].

**Work practice controls.**

- Wash hands immediately after removing gloves and after contact with blood or other potentially infectious materials. See the hand hygiene policy.
- Do not bend, recap or remove contaminated needles unless no alternative is feasible; if required, use a one-handed technique or a mechanical device.
- Do not eat, drink, smoke, apply cosmetics or handle contact lenses where exposure is likely.
- Place specimens in leakproof containers for collection, handling and transport.

**Personal protective equipment.** [Organization name] provides gloves, gowns, face shields, masks and eye protection at no cost, in appropriate sizes, at [locations]. Employees remove PPE before leaving the work area and place it in [designated containers].

**Housekeeping.** Work surfaces are decontaminated with [EPA-registered disinfectant] after procedures and after any spill. Regulated waste is handled according to [the regulated waste procedure] and state law. Contaminated laundry is [handled as described].

### 5. Hepatitis B vaccination

Hepatitis B vaccination is offered at no cost, after training and within 10 working days of initial assignment, to every employee with occupational exposure, unless the employee has had the complete series, antibody testing shows immunity, or the vaccine is medically contraindicated. An employee who declines signs the declination statement in Appendix A of the standard, and may change their mind later.

### 6. Post-exposure evaluation and follow-up

An employee who has an exposure incident:

1. Washes the area with soap and water, or flushes mucous membranes with water.
2. Reports the incident immediately to [title, phone number].
3. Is offered a confidential medical evaluation and follow-up by [provider], at no cost, including testing of the source individual's blood where consent can be obtained and the law allows.

[Title] documents the route of exposure and the circumstances, and reviews the incident with [the safety committee] to find what failed and what to change.

### 7. Communication of hazards and training

Containers of regulated waste and of blood or other potentially infectious materials carry the biohazard label or are red. Employees with occupational exposure are trained at initial assignment, at least annually thereafter, and whenever tasks change. Training records are kept for three years.

### 8. Recordkeeping

[Title] keeps medical records for each employee with occupational exposure for the duration of employment plus 30 years, confidentially and separately from personnel files. [If you keep an OSHA 300 log: Percutaneous injuries from contaminated sharps are recorded in the sharps injury log.]

### 9. Annual review and safer devices

[Title] reviews this plan at least once a year. Each review records the safer medical devices considered, those adopted and why, and the names of the frontline employees who gave input.

### Approval

This plan was reviewed and approved by [names and titles] on [date].

## How to adapt it

- **Be specific in section 3.** OSHA inspectors read the exposure determination first. Name the real job titles in your organization.
- **Name the devices you evaluated.** The annual review is weakest when it says only "no changes." Record the devices you looked at and why you chose what you did.
- **Match state law.** Some states run their own OSHA-approved plans with additional requirements.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often must an exposure control plan be updated?

At least annually, and whenever new or modified tasks and procedures, or new or revised positions, affect occupational exposure (29 CFR 1910.1030(c)(1)(iv)).

### Does a small medical office need an exposure control plan?

Yes, if any employee has occupational exposure to blood or other potentially infectious materials. The standard applies regardless of the number of employees.

### Must employees be able to see the plan?

Yes. Under §1910.1030(c)(1)(iii), the plan must be accessible to employees under the access rules in 29 CFR 1910.1020(e): in a reasonable time, place and manner, and if the employer can't provide it within 15 working days, it must tell the employee why and when they will have it.
