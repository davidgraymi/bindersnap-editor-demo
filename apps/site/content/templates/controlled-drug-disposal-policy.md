Few hospice policies are read by as many people as this one. The CMS hospice rule doesn't only require you to have it. It requires you to **hand a copy to the family** and talk them through it when controlled drugs are first ordered.

Under [42 CFR §418.106(e)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-418.106), "the hospice must have written policies and procedures for the management and disposal of controlled drugs in the patient's home." When controlled drugs are first ordered, the hospice must:

1. **Provide a copy** of the written policies and procedures to the patient or representative and family.
2. **Discuss** the policies and procedures for safe use and disposal with them, in a language and manner they understand.
3. **Document** in the clinical record that the policies were provided and discussed.

A hospice that provides inpatient care directly in its own facility has more to do. It must dispose of controlled drugs according to its policy and state and federal requirements, and keep current and accurate records of the receipt and disposition of all controlled drugs (§418.106(e)(2)(ii)). Its storage and discrepancy rules are in §418.106(e)(3).

## Federal disposal law changed in 2018

Section 3222 of the SUPPORT for Patients and Communities Act amended the Controlled Substances Act. Under conditions, it lets a qualified hospice's licensed employees, such as physicians and nurses, dispose of a patient's lawfully dispensed controlled substances in the home, for example after the patient's death, unless state law prohibits it. States vary widely. Some add their own requirements and some restrict the practice. Write section 5 of the template only after you have read your state's law and the hospice's own pharmacy and DEA guidance.

## The template

### [Hospice name] Management and Disposal of Controlled Drugs in the Patient's Home

- **Policy owner:** [Director of Clinical Services]
- **Approved by:** [Medical director, administrator, consultant pharmacist]
- **Effective date:** [Date]
- **Last reviewed:** [Date]

### 1. Purpose

To keep controlled drugs in the home safe from misuse, diversion and accidental exposure, and to tell patients and families how they will be managed and disposed of, in accordance with 42 CFR §418.106(e)(2) and [state law].

### 2. When controlled drugs are first ordered

The admitting or attending nurse:

1. Gives the patient or representative and family a copy of the family handout, "Keeping Controlled Medicines Safe at Home" (Attachment A), which states this policy in plain language.
2. Explains the handout in a language and manner the family understands, using [an interpreter service] when needed, and answers questions.
3. Documents in the clinical record that the policy was provided and discussed, who received it, and any interpreter used.

### 3. Safe storage and use in the home

The nurse teaches the family to:

- Store controlled medicines out of sight and reach of children, visitors and pets, [in a lockbox if provided or recommended].
- Give medicines only as prescribed, and keep track of doses given [using the medication log].
- Never share medicines or use them for anyone else.
- Call the hospice at [number] if medicines are missing, used up early, or the patient's pain changes.

At each visit, the nurse [reviews the medication log, counts controlled drugs as the hospice requires, and documents the count]. A suspected loss or diversion is reported to [the Director of Clinical Services] the same day and handled under [the diversion policy].

### 4. When controlled drugs are no longer needed

Controlled drugs are no longer needed when the patient dies, when the medication is discontinued, or when it has expired.

### 5. Disposal

[Write this section to match your state law and your hospice's authority under the Controlled Substances Act as amended by the SUPPORT Act. Common elements include:]

- Who may dispose: [licensed hospice nurses and physicians who have completed disposal training], or the family with the nurse present.
- How: [an approved drug-deactivation pouch or container], following the manufacturer's directions, or [a DEA-authorized collection site or take-back program].
- When the family refuses disposal: [document the refusal, provide disposal instructions and take-back locations].
- Documentation: the drug name, strength and quantity disposed of, the method, the date and time, and the names of the person disposing and any witness, recorded in the clinical record.

### 6. Education and competency

Nurses are trained on this policy and on family teaching at orientation and [annually]. [Title] audits [number] records each [quarter] for documentation that the policy was provided, discussed and followed.

### 7. Attachments

Attachment A — Family handout: "Keeping Controlled Medicines Safe at Home"
Attachment B — Controlled drug disposal record

### Approval

Reviewed and approved by [names and titles] on [date].

## How to adapt it

- **Write the family handout first.** It's what families read and what surveyors compare with the policy. Keep it short, at a plain reading level, and in the languages your patients speak.
- **Get pharmacy input.** Your consultant or contract pharmacy should review section 5 and the disposal products you use.
- **Keep the handout and the policy in step.** When you change one, change the other in the same revision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a hospice have to give families a copy of its controlled drug policy?

Yes. When controlled drugs are first ordered, 42 CFR §418.106(e)(2)(i) requires the hospice to give a copy of its written policies and procedures on managing and disposing of controlled drugs to the patient or representative and family, discuss them, and document that it did.

### Can hospice nurses dispose of a patient's medications after death?

Federal law has allowed it since the SUPPORT Act of 2018, for qualified hospice employees and under conditions, unless state law prohibits it. Check your state's rules and write your policy to match them.

### Where should the family dispose of medications?

Follow the method your policy sets: commonly a drug-deactivation product, a DEA-authorized collection site or a take-back event. Your state may set the options.
