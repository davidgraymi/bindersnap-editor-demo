**Short answer:** list every policy with its owner and its last review date, sort out the ones a rule puts on a fixed cycle, have each owner read theirs against current practice and current rules, send every policy through approval whether it changed or not, and keep a dated record of who reviewed which version. The reading is the easy part. Most reviews that fail a survey were done and can't be shown.

## Before you start: which policies have a cycle set by rule

Most federal rules say only that policies must be current. A few set a cycle, and those can't slip.

| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Exposure control plan                                                                     | Any employer with occupational exposure to blood | At least annually                              | [29 CFR 1910.1030(c)(1)(iv)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/section-1910.1030)                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Patient acceptance-to-service policy                                                      | Home health agencies                             | Maintained through an annual review            | [42 CFR 484.105(i)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-484.105)                                                                                                                                                                                             |

| Emergency plan, policies and procedures, communication plan, training and testing program | ASCs, home health, hospice, RHCs and FQHCs       | Each at least every 2 years                    | [42 CFR 416.54](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-416.54), [484.102](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-484.102), [418.113](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-418.113), [491.12](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-491.12) |

Your state and your accreditor usually add to this list. An annual review of everything covers all of them, which is why most small providers choose it. See [how often policies must be reviewed](/requirements/how-often-to-review-policies) for the full table.

## The eight steps

1. **Make the list.** Every policy, its owner, its approvers, and the date it was last reviewed. If you don't have one, a [policy manual index](/manuals) for your kind of provider is a place to start, and its Word download has the columns.
2. **Mark what changed outside.** New or amended rules, new accreditor standards, a survey finding, a [plan of correction](/glossary/plan-of-correction), an incident. Each points at specific policies that need more than a read-through.
3. **Mark what changed inside.** New services, new equipment, a new medical director, a new site. A policy can be correct on paper and wrong for the way you now work.
4. **Send each policy to its owner with one question:** does this describe what we do, and what the rules now require? Give a deadline. Two weeks is enough for most.
5. **Revise where needed.** Keep the old wording. You need to be able to show what the policy said before the review.
6. **Approve every policy, changed or not.** The approvers in your [policy on policies](/templates/policy-review-and-approval) approve the revisions, and approve the unchanged policies again. The second half is the one that gets skipped.
7. **Tell staff what changed.** A list of the revised policies with a line on each is enough for most. The emergency preparedness rule goes further: when those policies and procedures are "significantly updated", staff must be trained on the update (for example [42 CFR 416.54(d)(1)(v)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/section-416.54)).
8. **Report to the governing body.** How many policies were reviewed, how many changed, and which are overdue. It is responsible for the policies, and this is how it shows it looked.

## What to keep as proof

For each policy, a surveyor can ask three things about the review. Keep the answer to each.

- **Who reviewed it.** A name and a role, matching the approver your policy on policies requires.
- **When.** A date. "Reviewed 2026" in a footer is not a date, and nobody's name is on it.
- **What they reviewed.** The exact version. If the review led to a revision, keep both: the version reviewed and the version approved.

The OSHA rule shows how literal this can get. The annual review of an exposure control plan must itself document the consideration of safer medical devices, so "no changes" on its own doesn't meet it. The [exposure control plan template](/templates/exposure-control-plan) has a section for it.

## Spreading the work

A manual of 150 policies reviewed in one month buries the people who have to read them. Two ways around it, both of which still meet an annual cycle:

- **By section, by month.** Infection control in January, medications in February, and so on. Each policy is still reviewed once a year, on its own anniversary.
- **By owner.** Each owner gets their policies in one batch, staggered so the medical director is not approving everything in the same week.

Whichever you pick, write it into the policy on policies, so the cycle a surveyor measures you against is the one you actually run.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do all healthcare policies have to be reviewed annually?

No federal rule says so for every policy. A few policies have an annual cycle set by rule, such as the OSHA exposure control plan and a home health agency's acceptance-to-service policy. Many states and accreditors expect annual review of more, and most small providers review everything annually because one cycle is easier to keep and to prove.

### Who should do the annual policy review?

The owner of each policy reads it, and the approvers named in your policy on policies approve it. For some policies a rule names the reviewer: a rural health clinic's patient care policies are reviewed by a group that includes a physician, a PA or NP, and someone from outside the clinic's staff.

### What if a policy has no changes?

It still needs a dated record that the right person reviewed that version. Approve it again and keep the record.

### How long does an annual policy review take?

It depends on the size of the manual and how much changed. Spreading it by section across the year turns one large job into twelve small ones.
